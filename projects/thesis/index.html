Homogeneous black holes in 5D.

Master's thesis in theoretical physics (University of Milano-Bicocca, November 2026) — can a black hole have a horizon shaped like one of Thurston's exotic geometries, and what does the spacetime around it look like? Supervisors: Prof. Silke Klemm and Prof. Silvia Penati.

The question

In our four-dimensional world, Hawking proved that the horizon of a black hole must be a sphere — but the theorem relies on a cosmological constant that is positive or zero. In anti-de Sitter (AdS) space, the negatively curved universe at the heart of the AdS/CFT correspondence (which links gravity to quantum field theories), the rules are much looser: horizons can be flat, hyperbolic, or have far stranger shapes.

In five dimensions the horizon is a three-dimensional space, and Thurston’s classification gives eight possible model geometries for it. The familiar ones (sphere, flat, hyperbolic) are well studied; my thesis focuses on the more exotic twisted ones, Nil and Sol. I studied four families of black holes in five-dimensional gauged supergravity, combining analytic techniques for the first three with a numerical approach for the fourth.

Part 1 Zooming into AdS black holes

I applied a scaling limit — the Inönü–Wigner contraction — to the Schwarzschild–AdS5 and Reissner–Nordström–AdS5 black holes. It zooms onto one point of the horizon while boosting it to the speed of light, turning the spherical SU(2) symmetry into the Nil one.

Result The limit is still an exact solution of the field equations: a gravitational wave with flat wave fronts (carrying an electromagnetic wave along with it in the charged case). Because of the cosmological constant it is not a standard pp-wave but a plane-fronted wave of Kundt type, the AdS analogue of the Siklos waves.

Part 2 A black hole in a Gödel universe

The Gödel universe is a rotating spacetime famous for allowing closed timelike curves — paths that return to their own past. I studied how light moves around a black hole placed in it, and then applied the same contraction.

Result After the contraction the horizon disappears and the spacetime is no longer a black hole. Once the Nil directions are made compact, a closed timelike curve passes through every point, whatever the mass of the black hole and the size of the compact directions.

Part 3 The magnetic Sol black hole

The core of the thesis is a black hole whose horizon has Sol geometry, supported by a magnetic field of strength p. No analytic solution with all the metric functions varying was known, and none of the ansätze I tried worked, so I built the solution numerically: a series expansion near the horizon, then a Runge–Kutta integration outwards, using one of the Einstein equations as a check on the error (about 10−9).

Scanning the parameters, and locating the boundaries with a bisection algorithm, I found that everything is controlled by a critical field p* = α₀√−Λ, which does not depend on the temperature of the black hole:

p < p*

A regular black hole, asymptotically AdS5 with boundary ℝt × Sol — a new family generalising the known solutions.

p = p*

Exactly the analytic solution already known in the literature, recovered numerically.

p > p*

Two Sol directions collapse at a finite radius: a curvature singularity with a power-law structure (exponent 2/3), derived analytically and checked against the numerics.

p < plow

In part of parameter space the third direction collapses instead, so regular solutions exist only for plow < p < p*.

The extremal (zero-temperature) case

Here the geometry near the horizon is AdS2 × Sol, and small deformations of it grow with an irrational power of the distance, k₊ = ½(−1 + √(35/3)) ≈ 1.2078. The solution therefore cannot be written as an ordinary Taylor series: I built the correct double series and matched it numerically with the Fefferman–Graham expansion at infinity, obtaining the horizon size as a function of the magnetic field.
