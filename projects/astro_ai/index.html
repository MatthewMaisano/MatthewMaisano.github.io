AI applied to astrophysics.

Statistics, machine learning and neural networks on real and simulated astrophysical data — 20 notebooks with their results.

Code: github.com/MatthewMaisano/Astrostatistics-Laboratory

The idea. Modern astrophysics produces far more data than anyone can look at by hand: millions of galaxies, thousands of gravitational-wave candidates, light curves of transient events. This collection gathers the exercises I solved during the Astrostatistics and Machine Learning course at the University of Milano-Bicocca (Prof. Davide Gerosa), going from the basics of probability and Monte Carlo methods to Bayesian inference, unsupervised learning and neural networks. Each card below summarises, in plain words, what problem I tackled and what came out of it; the button opens the full Python notebook with code and plots.

Tools. Python · NumPy · SciPy · scikit-learn · PyTorch · emcee · dynesty · astroML · pandas · Matplotlib

Probability & Monte Carlo methods

Simulating randomness to answer questions that are hard to solve with pen and paper.

L02 The Monty Hall problem
SimulationProbability

What I did. Simulated 1,000 rounds of the famous game-show puzzle (three doors, one prize) for three strategies: keep the first door, switch, or pick again at random.

Result. Switching wins 2/3 of the time, staying only 1/3, a random re-pick 1/2 — the simulation confirms the counter-intuitive answer.

L03 Monte Carlo integration
Monte CarloIntegration

What I did. Computed an integral by averaging random draws from a Gaussian instead of solving it analytically, and studied how the error shrinks as the number of draws grows.

Result. The estimates cluster around the exact value (2σ⁴ = 0.125) and their scatter falls steadily with more samples, as Monte Carlo theory predicts.

L04 Cloning quasars
SamplingSDSS data

What I did. Took the redshifts of 10,000 real quasars from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and generated 100,000 synthetic “clones” with two techniques: rejection sampling and inverse-CDF sampling.

Result. Both methods reproduce the shape of the real redshift distribution — a way to create realistic fake catalogues from any measured distribution.

Extra Volume of an N-dimensional sphere
Monte CarloCurse of dimensionality

What I did. Threw random points into a cube in 1 to 20 dimensions and counted how many land inside the sphere that fits in it.

Result. The fraction drops from 78% in 2D to essentially zero above ~12 dimensions: in high dimensions almost all the volume sits in the “corners” — the curse of dimensionality that makes machine learning on many features hard.

Statistical inference

Extracting physical parameters — and their uncertainties — from noisy measurements.

L05 Maximum likelihood on noisy data
Maximum likelihoodFisher matrix

What I did. Combined 100 measurements of the same quantity, each with a different error bar (heteroscedastic data), by multiplying their likelihoods.

Result. Best estimate μ = 0.985 ± 0.017 (true value 1), with the uncertainty obtained from the curvature of the log-likelihood (Fisher information).

L06 How many black-hole populations?
Gaussian mixturesModel selection

What I did. Fitted the mass distribution of a simulated black-hole catalogue with a mixture of 1 to 10 Gaussians, using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to choose the number of components; also estimated the density with a cross-validated kernel density estimator.

Result. The AIC is minimised with 3 components: the data are best explained by three distinct formation channels.

Open notebook
L07 Black-hole irreducible mass
Histograms & KDEKS test

What I did. Computed the “irreducible mass” of spinning black holes (the part of the mass that can never be extracted) for random spins, compared histogram rules (equal-count bins, Freedman–Diaconis) and kernel density estimates, and used the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to see which ingredient dominates the result.

Result. When the mass spread is small the spin shapes the distribution; as the spread grows the irreducible mass simply follows the mass distribution (KS distance → 0).

Bayesian inference & MCMC

Letting the data update our beliefs, and exploring parameter space with Markov chains.

L10 Markov chains: weather and stock market
Markov chains

What I did. Simulated two Markov chains: a two-state weather model (clear/cloudy) and a three-state stock market (bull/bear/stagnant), tracking how often each state occurs.

Result. Both converge to their stationary distribution: 83% clear days; 62.5% bull, 31% bear, 6% stagnant market — matching the analytic values.

L11 Fitting a cosmic transient with emcee
MCMCemcee

What I did. Modelled the light curve of a transient (a sudden burst followed by exponential decay) and sampled the 4 model parameters with the emcee MCMC sampler, checking convergence via autocorrelation times and acceptance fraction (0.54).

Result. The posterior pins down the background level (≈10.3), the burst time (≈50) and the decay rate (≈0.12), with full uncertainties and correlations (corner plot).

L12 Model comparison with nested sampling
Nested samplingBayesian evidence

Result. The Bayesian evidence favours the burst model by a factor ≈ 235 — strong, quantitative evidence for one physical model over the other.

Unsupervised learning

Finding structure in data without being told the answer.

L13 Reading handwritten digits
ClassificationIsomap

What I did. Trained a logistic-regression classifier on 1,797 images of handwritten digits (8×8 pixels), with and without first compressing them to 2 dimensions with Isomap.

Result. 96% accuracy on unseen digits using all 64 pixels; 72% after compressing to just 2 numbers per image — a clear view of the information lost by dimensionality reduction.

L15 Classifying stars without labels
PCAClustering

What I did. Took 240 stars described by temperature, luminosity, radius and magnitude, standardised the data, compressed it with Principal Component Analysis and clustered it — without using the true star types.

Result. Two components keep 98% of the information (76% in the first alone) and mean-shift finds 6 clusters — exactly the number of real star types (dwarfs, main sequence, giants…).

Regression

Fitting curves through data without over- or under-fitting.

L17 Supernova Hubble diagram: polynomial regression
RegressionCross-validation

What I did. Fitted the distance of 100 supernovae as a function of redshift with straight lines and polynomials of degree 1 to 8, comparing χ² and 10-fold cross-validation errors.

Result. Cross-validation picks a degree-5 polynomial: higher degrees start chasing noise (overfitting), lower ones miss the curvature.

L18 Supernova distances with Gaussian Processes
Gaussian ProcessesCross-validation

What I did. Fitted the same supernova data with a non-parametric Gaussian Process, scanning the kernel length-scale and choosing it by cross-validation; started a cosmological fit with emcee.

Result. Too short a length-scale overfits wildly, too long underfits; the cross-validated choice gives a smooth curve with uncertainty bands.

Supervised learning & neural networks

Teaching algorithms to recognise classes of astrophysical objects.

L19 Galaxies vs quasars from their colours
ClassificationSDSS data

What I did. Used the colours (differences of u, g, r, i, z magnitudes) of 50,000 SDSS objects to separate galaxies from quasars with Quadratic Discriminant Analysis and a Gaussian-mixture Bayes classifier.

Result. ≈ 98% accuracy (cross-validated), recovering ~93% of quasars with only ~6–10% contamination.

L20 Which black-hole mergers will we detect?
t-SNEDecision trees

What I did. Took a large simulated catalogue of binary black-hole mergers and predicted whether a gravitational-wave detector would see each one (signal-to-noise > 12) from inclination, redshift and mass ratio, using t-SNE and bagged decision trees.

Result. 82.5% of detectable events are correctly identified, with 13% contamination.

Result. 93.75% accuracy on unseen events (750 / 800) — better than the decision-tree approach.
