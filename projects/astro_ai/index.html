Modern astrophysics produces far more data than anyone can look at by hand: millions of galaxies, thousands of gravitational-wave candidates, light curves of transient events. Each card below summarises what problem I tackled and what came out of it; the link opens the full notebook with code and plots.

Probability & Monte Carlo methods

Simulating randomness to answer questions that are hard to solve with pen and paper.

The Monty Hall problem

L02
  • Simulation
  • Probability

Simulated 1,000 rounds of the famous game-show puzzle (three doors, one prize) for three strategies: keep the first door, switch, or pick again at random.

Result. Switching wins 2/3 of the time, staying only 1/3, a random re-pick 1/2 — the simulation confirms the counter-intuitive answer.

Monte Carlo integration

L03
  • Monte Carlo
  • Integration

Computed an integral by averaging random draws from a Gaussian instead of solving it analytically, and studied how the error shrinks as the number of draws grows.

Result. The estimates cluster around the exact value (2σ⁴ = 0.125) and their scatter falls steadily with more samples, as Monte Carlo theory predicts.

Cloning quasars

L04
  • Sampling
  • SDSS data

Took the redshifts of 10,000 real quasars from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and generated 100,000 synthetic “clones” with two techniques: rejection sampling and inverse-CDF sampling.

Result. Both methods reproduce the shape of the real redshift distribution — a way to create realistic fake catalogues from any measured distribution.

Volume of an N-dimensional sphere

Extra
  • Monte Carlo
  • Curse of dimensionality

Threw random points into a cube in 1 to 20 dimensions and counted how many land inside the sphere that fits in it.

Result. The fraction drops from 78% in 2D to essentially zero above ~12 dimensions: in high dimensions almost all the volume sits in the “corners” — the curse of dimensionality that makes machine learning on many features hard.

Statistical inference

Extracting physical parameters — and their uncertainties — from noisy measurements.

Maximum likelihood on noisy data

L05
  • Maximum likelihood
  • Fisher matrix

Combined 100 measurements of the same quantity, each with a different error bar (heteroscedastic data), by multiplying their likelihoods.

Result. Best estimate μ = 0.985 ± 0.017 (true value 1), with the uncertainty obtained from the curvature of the log-likelihood (Fisher information).

How many black-hole populations?

L06
  • Gaussian mixtures
  • Model selection

Fitted the mass distribution of a simulated black-hole catalogue with a mixture of 1 to 10 Gaussians, using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to choose the number of components; also estimated the density with a cross-validated kernel density estimator.

Result. The AIC is minimised with 3 components: the data are best explained by three distinct formation channels.

Black-hole irreducible mass

L07
  • Histograms & KDE
  • KS test

Computed the “irreducible mass” of spinning black holes (the part of the mass that can never be extracted) for random spins, compared histogram rules (equal-count bins, Freedman–Diaconis) and kernel density estimates, and used the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to see which ingredient dominates the result.

Result. When the mass spread is small the spin shapes the distribution; as the spread grows the irreducible mass simply follows the mass distribution (KS distance → 0).

Bayesian inference & MCMC

Letting the data update our beliefs, and exploring parameter space with Markov chains.

Markov chains: weather and stock market

L10
  • Markov chains

Simulated two Markov chains: a two-state weather model (clear/cloudy) and a three-state stock market (bull/bear/stagnant), tracking how often each state occurs.

Result. Both converge to their stationary distribution: 83% clear days; 62.5% bull, 31% bear, 6% stagnant market — matching the analytic values.

Fitting a cosmic transient with emcee

L11
  • MCMC
  • emcee

Modelled the light curve of a transient (a sudden burst followed by exponential decay) and sampled the 4 model parameters with the emcee MCMC sampler, checking convergence via autocorrelation times and acceptance fraction (0.54).

Result. The posterior pins down the background level (≈10.3), the burst time (≈50) and the decay rate (≈0.12), with full uncertainties and correlations (corner plot).

Model comparison with nested sampling

L12
  • Nested sampling
  • Bayesian evidence

Re-analysed the same transient with the dynesty nested sampler and compared two hypotheses: a sharp burst with exponential decay vs a symmetric Gaussian bump.

Result. The Bayesian evidence favours the burst model by a factor ≈ 235 — strong, quantitative evidence for one physical model over the other.

Unsupervised learning

Finding structure in data without being told the answer.

Reading handwritten digits

L13
  • Classification
  • Isomap

Trained a logistic-regression classifier on 1,797 images of handwritten digits (8×8 pixels), with and without first compressing them to 2 dimensions with Isomap.

Result. 96% accuracy on unseen digits using all 64 pixels; 72% after compressing to just 2 numbers per image — a clear view of the information lost by dimensionality reduction.

Classifying stars without labels

L15
  • PCA
  • Clustering

Took 240 stars described by temperature, luminosity, radius and magnitude, standardised the data, compressed it with Principal Component Analysis and clustered it — without using the true star types.

Result. Two components keep 98% of the information (76% in the first alone) and mean-shift finds 6 clusters — exactly the number of real star types (dwarfs, main sequence, giants…).

Regression

Fitting curves through data without over- or under-fitting.

Supernova Hubble diagram: polynomial regression

L17
  • Regression
  • Cross-validation

Fitted the distance of 100 supernovae as a function of redshift with straight lines and polynomials of degree 1 to 8, comparing χ² and 10-fold cross-validation errors.

Result. Cross-validation picks a degree-5 polynomial: higher degrees start chasing noise (overfitting), lower ones miss the curvature.

Supernova distances with Gaussian Processes

L18
  • Gaussian Processes
  • Cross-validation

Fitted the same supernova data with a non-parametric Gaussian Process, scanning the kernel length-scale and choosing it by cross-validation; started a cosmological fit with emcee.

Result. Too short a length-scale overfits wildly, too long underfits; the cross-validated choice gives a smooth curve with uncertainty bands.

Supervised learning & neural networks

Teaching algorithms to recognise classes of astrophysical objects.

Galaxies vs quasars from their colours

L19
  • Classification
  • SDSS data

Used the colours (differences of u, g, r, i, z magnitudes) of 50,000 SDSS objects to separate galaxies from quasars with Quadratic Discriminant Analysis and a Gaussian-mixture Bayes classifier.

Result. ≈ 98% accuracy (cross-validated), recovering ~93% of quasars with only ~6–10% contamination.

Which black-hole mergers will we detect?

L20
  • t-SNE
  • Decision trees

Took a large simulated catalogue of binary black-hole mergers and predicted whether a gravitational-wave detector would see each one (signal-to-noise > 12) from inclination, redshift and mass ratio, using t-SNE and bagged decision trees.

Result. 82.5% of detectable events are correctly identified, with 13% contamination.

Neural network for gravitational-wave detectability

L22

  • PyTorch

Built and trained from scratch a small neural network in PyTorch (3 inputs → 6 hidden neurons → 2 outputs) on the same black-hole problem, using stochastic gradient descent for 1,000 epochs.

Result. 93.75% accuracy on unseen events (750 / 800) — better than the decision-tree approach.
